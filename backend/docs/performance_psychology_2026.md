# Performance Psychology & Adjudication Framework (2026 Research)

This document synthesizes deep research into the psychology, performance, and adjudication of Music and Dance, extending the "Adjudication Companion" model previously developed for Drama.

## 1. Music (Vocal, Instrumental, Choral)

### First Principle: The Performer as a Bio-Feedback System
A musician is not just executing a score; they are managing a high-frequency feedback loop between auditory intention and physical execution.
*   **Dual-Process Monitoring:** The expert musician maintains "Global Flow" (the emotional arc) while performing "Micro-Correction" (adjusting intonation, breath, or fingering in real-time).
*   **Embodied Cognition:** Music is a physical act. Vocalists use the entire body as a resonator; instrumentalists extend their body schema into the instrument.
*   **Attentional Control:** Adjudicators should look for "divided attention" that isn't "fragmented." A performer who is purely technical lacks "Soul"; one who is purely emotional lacks "Control."

### Music Performance Anxiety (MPA) and Flow
*   **Flow State:** Defined by the challenge-skill balance. If a piece is too hard, anxiety peaks; too easy, boredom peaks. Flow happens in the "sweet spot."
*   **Somatic vs. Cognitive Anxiety:** Trembling hands (somatic) can happen to even great performers, but "cognitive interference" (worrying about the adjudicator) is what usually breaks the performance.
*   **Self-Efficacy:** The single strongest predictor of success. A performer's belief that they *can* hit the high note often determines if they *do*.

## 2. Dance (Acro, Contemporary, Ballet)

### First Principle: Proprioception as Social Signal
In dance, the body *is* the text. The audience reads the dancer's intent through the geometry of their form.
*   **Spatial Command:** A dancer's psychological "Theory of Mind" is expressed through how they occupy space. Stillness in dance is as powerful as silence in music.
*   **Line and Form:** The audience perceives "Intentionality" through the clarity of the lines. A "broken line" often signals a break in psychological focus.

### Performance Psychology in Dance
*   **Flow in Movement:** Unlike music, dance involves total-body displacement, which can induce flow more easily but also carries higher "Visual Evaluation Anxiety" (body image).
*   **Causal Coherence:** Does the jump come from a "Need to fly" (emotional) or a "Need to execute step 4" (mechanical)?
*   **Narrative Continuity:** The ability to sustain a character's internal logic through complex physical exertion.

## 3. The Adjudicator's Role: Developmental Intervention
Following the NEA philosophy, adjudication is a "Learning Interaction."
*   **Avoid Identity Labels:** "You are a messy dancer" → "Your current performance shows inconsistent spatial clarity."
*   **Competence, Agency, Next Challenge:**
    *   **Competence:** Acknowledge what was controlled.
    *   **Agency:** Identify the performer's specific successful choice.
    *   **Next Challenge:** Provide a psychologically safe developmental path.

---

## 4. Bias Control in Music and Dance
*   **The "Virtuosity Halo":** Adjudicators often overlook poor phrasing if the technique is fast/loud.
*   **The "Visual Bias":** In music, we often judge by how a performer *looks* like they are feeling, rather than the sound.
*   **Adversarial Self-Checking:** The AI must ask: "What did I miss because I liked their tone?"


---

## 5. Adjudicator Remarks: Best Practices (all categories)
- Evidence first, then interpretation; then recommendation. Separate what was observed from what it means and what to do next.
- Be specific: cite bars/measures, beats, or moments; quote a phrase/gesture; point to a spatial choice.
- Acknowledge constraints (accompaniment, stage, mic, footwear, time) and avoid penalizing unavoidable limitations.
- Admit subjectivity: frame notes as an expert perspective, not absolute truth. Invite reflection rather than dictate.
- Keep the performer-teacher ecosystem in mind: phrase feedback so it supports teaching/learning, not undermines it.
- Use psychologically safe language: avoid identity labels; focus on trainable skills and choices.

Source examples: National Theatre School Drama Festival Adjudicators' Guide (2020) and NEA adjudication philosophy.

## 6. Music: Domain specifics
### 6.1 Vocal (classical, musical theatre, contemporary)
- Breath management → stable subglottal pressure; onset type (soft/firm/aspirate) matched to style and lyric meaning.
- Registration → negotiated passaggio; even timbre across range without forcing; stylistic vibrato control (amplitude/rate) or straight tone where appropriate.
- Diction → intelligibility without jaw/tongue tension; vowel purity; consonant energy proportional to tempo and style.
- Phrasing → textual meaning drives emphasis and caesura; dynamic shaping supports dramatic narrative.
- Anxiety indicators → vibrato instability, jaw tremor, breath stacking; treat as state, not trait; coach recovery strategies.

### 6.2 Instrumental (strings, winds, brass, piano, percussion)
- Tone production → bow speed/pressure/contact point; embouchure stability; resonance; stick choice and stroke type.
- Intonation → reference frames (open strings, drone, just vs equal temperament compromises); lip/bow/hand corrections.
- Articulation → clarity of attacks/releases; length relative to style; consistency within gestures.
- Rhythm/tempo → internal pulse; micro-timing at phrase ends; flexibility vs rushing.
- Stagecraft → tuning etiquette, count-in confidence, page turns, pedal noise, physical economy.

### 6.3 Choral/Ensemble
- Blend vs individuality → vowel unification, matched resonance strategies, dynamic balance by function (melody vs harmony).
- Entraining attention → breath together, unified onsets, shared phrase intentions.
- Listening → active correction during sustains; tuning of thirds/sixths; conductor-performer feedback loop.
- Text intelligibility vs legato trade-offs → articulate without chopping line.

## 7. Dance: Domain specifics
### 7.1 Technique
- Alignment → stacked joints, neutral pelvis, scapular control; weight transfer clarity; turnout integrity from hip.
- Power and control → landing mechanics (deceleration, foot articulation), safe spine in acro skills; eccentric control.
- Line and extension → energy beyond extremities; uninterrupted pathways; hand and head finish.

### 7.2 Musicality and Composition
- Rhythm → groove, swing, syncopation accuracy; match of dynamic accents to musical form.
- Spatial design → floor patterns, levels, facings; negative space awareness; audience sightline logic.
- Transitions → movement grammar between skills; avoid “trick chaining” without connective phrasing.

### 7.3 Performance Psychology
- Presence without over-activation → stillness as attention tool; gaze as social signal aligned to convention.
- Anxiety signs → rigidity, reduced range, shallow breath; distinguish capacity from current state.

## 8. Developmental standards by age/phase (guide)
- Early (≤ Grade 3): clarity, basic control, confident starts/finishes, imagination, simple phrasing.
- Middle (Grades 4–7): consistency, variation with intention, coordination, responsiveness, stylistic awareness.
- Senior (Grades 8–12): integration of technique and interpretation, subtext/nuance, dynamic risk with control, ensemble leadership.

## 9. Rubric pattern (all domains)
- Technique: controllability and reliability under pressure.
- Intention: clarity of purpose at phrase/beat level.
- Coherence: causal links between choices; motivated transitions.
- Communication: audience receives meaning without over-explanation.
- Appropriateness: stylistic, age, and category fit.
